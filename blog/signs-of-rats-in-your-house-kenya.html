Signs of Rats in Your House or Shop in Kenya

Updated 1 November 2026 · 8 min read

Quick answer: To confirm rats in your Kenyan house or shop, look for dark spindle-shaped droppings, fresh wood or plastic gnaw marks, greasy rub smudges along skirting boards, and shredded paper or cloth nests. Hearing scratching in the ceiling boards at night or smelling a stale, ammonia-like scent also points directly to rodent activity.

Dealing with unwanted pests inside a residential home, retail shop, or storehouse in Kenya can be stressful and disruptive. Rodents often move quietly behind stock, under kitchen cabinets, and inside roof voids before you ever see one in broad daylight. Recognizing the early physical clues they leave behind allows you to intervene before stock gets spoiled, electrical wiring is damaged, or hygiene is compromised. Here is a clear guide on how to spot the distinct signs of rats Kenya residents encounter, understand where they hide, and locate their entry routes.

What do rat droppings look like in a home or shop?

Finding droppings is often the very first visual warning that rodents have entered your premises. Rat droppings are distinct in size and shape compared to common house gecko or mouse droppings. While mouse pellets are small with pointed ends, rat droppings are notably larger, dark brown or black, oblong, and usually measure between ten and twenty millimetres in length with blunt or tapered ends.

Fresh droppings appear shiny, dark, and somewhat soft, while older droppings turn dull, grey, brittle, and dry over time. If you notice a cluster of fresh pellets near your kitchen dustbin, along the base of pantry shelves, or behind bags of dry food in a duka, rats are feeding actively in that area.

When inspecting or cleaning up these droppings, avoid sweeping them dry with a broom, as this can kick fine particles into the air. Instead, wear gloves, lightly dampen the area with water and household disinfectant, and wipe them up safely before discarding them into an outdoor bin.

  • Inspect dark corners under the sink and behind kitchen cookers.
  • Check along dark wall bases behind wholesale carton stacks.
  • Look for fresh, moist pellets that indicate active, current movement.

Why do I hear rats in ceiling spaces at night?

A very common complaint among homeowners and commercial shopkeepers is hearing rats in ceiling spaces after dark. Rodents are largely nocturnal creatures, meaning they remain hidden during noisy daytime business hours and emerge once the lights go out and foot traffic settles. When night falls, roof spaces become active corridors for them to move across the building.

The sounds you hear usually include persistent scratching on soft gypsum boards or timber brandering, scurrying across iron sheets, and occasional squeaking or thumping. Roof voids offer an ideal sanctuary because they are warm, undisturbed, and provide immediate pathways to the rest of the building through hollow wall gaps, electrical conduits, and ceiling trap doors.

If these noises are regular, rodents may be bringing food back into the ceiling or building communal nests among loose insulation, cardboard boxes, and ceiling timbers. Left unchecked, they can chew into ceiling wiring or soil the panels below.

How can you spot rat gnaw marks and grease marks?

Because rodent front teeth grow continuously throughout their lives, rats must gnaw on tough materials daily to keep them worn down. In homes and shops, this behaviour leaves unmistakable damage. Look for rough, paired tooth marks on food packaging, timber door frames, skirting boards, PVC water pipes, and flexible electrical conduit.

Fresh gnaw marks are usually light in colour, revealing the fresh raw wood or clean plastic underneath. As the marks age, they darken and collect dust. In commercial retail stores, gnawing frequently appears along the lower edges of maize flour packets, sugar bags, and the corners of corrugated storage cartons.

In addition to teeth marks, look for grease rubs, also known as smear marks. Rats have oily coats that collect dust and grime. Because they have poor eyesight, they navigate by muscle memory and hug walls as they travel. Over time, their bodies leave dark, greasy smudges along skirting boards, around door frames, and along overhead ceiling rafters where they squeeze through.

  • Check lower edges of wooden doors where rats try to squeeze under.
  • Look for darkened, waxy grease lines along wall skirting and pipes.
  • Examine electrical conduits for exposed wires or shredded plastic.

Where are rats entering your Kenyan house or retail shop?

Rats are agile climbers and capable of compressing their bodies through surprisingly narrow gaps. Identifying their entry points is essential if you want to keep them out permanently. In Kenya, building designs with corrugated iron roofing, external louvres, and open drainage spaces often provide easy access points.

Common entry spots include the gap beneath wooden external doors, spaces around poorly sealed water and waste pipes, open vent blocks, and uncapped conduit ends. If you run a commercial shop with a roller shutter, the uneven gap between the bottom metal rail and the rough floor apron is a frequent pathway for pests to slip in after closing time.

Outside the building, check the roofline where the iron sheets meet the fascia boards. Rats can scale rough plaster, climbing plants, or overhanging tree branches to enter directly through roof overlaps into the ceiling void. Burrow holes dug directly into the soil along your foundation slabs also indicate external rat burrows.

What should you check next to deal with them?

Once you have confirmed that rodents are present, the next step is assessing the extent of the problem and taking practical steps. Start by eliminating their food sources. Keep dry grains, flour, and shop inventory in rigid, sealed containers or metal drums, and ensure outside rubbish bins have tight lids.

Next, inspect all identified entry holes and block them using tough, rodent-resistant materials like cement, coarse wire mesh, or sheet metal so new pests cannot enter from the street or compound. After securing the physical perimeter, you can evaluate suitable control products.

Frequently asked questions

How can I tell the difference between rat and mouse droppings?

Rat droppings are much larger, usually measuring 10 to 20 mm with rounded or tapered ends, resembling dark olive pits. Mouse droppings are significantly smaller, measuring around 3 to 6 mm, with distinctly pointed tips, similar to small grains of dark rice.

Why do rats chew on household wiring and plastic pipes?

A rat's incisor teeth grow constantly, requiring regular gnawing on hard surfaces like timber, hard plastic, and PVC pipe insulation to file them down. Chewing wires is not for food, but it poses serious electrical short-circuit and fire risks in homes and shops.

What size of gap can a rat squeeze through to enter a building?

Adult rats can squeeze through openings as narrow as twenty millimetres, roughly the diameter of a Kenyan twenty-shilling coin. Young rats can slip through even smaller openings. Any unsealed pipe penetrations or loose roof gaps can serve as doorways.

Why do rats travel along walls instead of crossing the middle of the room?

Rats have poor eyesight and rely heavily on their sensitive whiskers and body fur to navigate by touch. Moving along skirting boards and walls provides them with security, hiding them from open sight and creating the dark grease smudges commonly seen along floor trim.

Where do rats typically build nests inside a commercial retail shop?

In retail shops, rats prefer undisturbed, dark spaces with easy access to nesting material. They frequently nest inside stacked cardboard cartons, behind bulk sacks of dry grain, under display counters, or directly in the roof void above the ceiling boards.
