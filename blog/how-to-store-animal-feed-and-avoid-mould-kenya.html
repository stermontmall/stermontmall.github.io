How to Store Animal Feed and Avoid Mould in Kenya

Updated 25 November 2026 · 7 min read

Quick answer: To store animal feed and prevent feed mould in Kenya, keep bags off bare concrete on wooden pallets, maintain continuous airflow around storage stacks, keep the area dry, and apply a strict first-in, first-out stock system. Ensure the room is fully rodent-proofed and inspect stored feed frequently for clumps, discoloration, or unusual odours.

Keeping livestock feed safe from moisture and pests is essential for farm productivity in Kenya. When feed gets damp or spoiled, it loses nutritional value and can harm your animals. By practicing proper feed storage Kenya farmers can keep feed fresh, reduce wastage, and maintain health standards across all seasons.

Why does feed mould develop during storage?

Feed mould is a major challenge for farmers and livestock managers across Kenya. Spores are naturally present in the environment, but they only grow into visible fungal colonies when environmental conditions allow. High relative humidity, warm ambient temperatures, and direct moisture exposure are the primary drivers of fungal growth in stored commercial feeds, grains, and custom mixes.

Moisture can enter storage facilities in several subtle ways. Concrete floors absorb moisture from the ground below, which quickly transfers into stacked feed bags. Leaking roofs, unsealed window frames, and poor ventilation also trap humid air inside the room. As heat builds up, condensation forms on the inner surface of bags, creating moist pockets where fungi thrive rapidly.

Spoiled feed not only loses vital energy, protein, and vitamin contents, but it can also release harmful fungal byproducts. Feeding damp or clumpy material to poultry, cattle, or pigs can lead to reduced feed intake, poor weight gain, and digestive distress. Understanding how to manage ambient conditions in your storage shed is the first step toward long-term feed protection.

  • Direct moisture from ground contact or roof leaks
  • High ambient humidity trapped inside unventilated spaces
  • Temperature fluctuations leading to internal bag condensation
  • Prolonged storage beyond recommended timelines

How should you elevate and ventilate stored feed?

Proper elevation is the most effective physical barrier against moisture absorption. Never place feed bags directly on bare concrete or earthen floors. Always stack bags on raised wooden pallets or plastic skids raised at least ten to fifteen centimeters off the floor. This clearance allows continuous air movement underneath the stack, preventing ground dampness from soaking into the bottom layers.

Ventilation around the perimeter of your storage area is equally critical when learning how to store animal feed safely. Stack bags in neat, interlocking rows while leaving a clear gap of at least thirty to fifty centimeters between the stacks and the surrounding walls. Wall clearance prevents moisture transfer from exterior walls during heavy rains and encourages airflow around every side of the pile.

Ensure your storage building features high-level air vents fitted with fine wire mesh. Mesh vents allow warm, humid air to escape while preventing wild birds and larger insects from entering. If you are stocking bulk nutrients alongside essential poultry feed premixes, keeping airflow consistent throughout the room ensures all bagged ingredients remain dry and stable over time.

How do you practice first-in, first-out stock management?

Inventory management plays a direct role in maintaining feed quality on any farm. The first-in, first-out principles dictate that the oldest stock in your storage room should always be consumed before opening newly delivered supplies. This rotation prevents older bags from sitting neglected in corners where moisture and pests can gradually accumulate.

To implement an effective rotation system, label every newly arrived stack or bag with its date of receipt using a bold permanent marker. Organize your floor layout so that newer shipments are loaded behind older stock on your pallets. When feeding your animals daily, workers should naturally pull from the front rows first, ensuring feed is used within its optimal freshness window.

Calculate your herd or flock consumption rate carefully before placing bulk orders. Buying feed in quantities that match your actual consumption schedule reduces storage duration and minimizes exposure to fluctuating humidity. Planning delivery schedules so that fresh stock arrives just as previous supplies are finishing keeps your feed supply safe and fresh.

How do you keep rodents and insects out of feed stores?

Pests are major contributors to feed spoilage and moisture contamination in Kenyan farm stores. Rats, mice, and grain insects chew through woven sacks, leaving open torn areas where atmospheric moisture enters. Furthermore, rodent droppings and urine introduce unwanted humidity and contamination directly into the feed, creating localized spots for fungal proliferation.

Rodent-proofing your storage shed requires sealing every visible entry point. Fill gaps around door frames, repair cracked masonry near floor levels, and install metal kick-plates on wooden doors. Combining solid structural proofing with proven rodent-proofing techniques ensures that pests cannot establish nesting sites beneath your pallets or between bag rows.

Cleanliness inside and outside the store is vital. Sweep up spilled grain and dust daily, as fallen feed attracts pests from surrounding fields. Before bringing in a major new delivery of raw grains or feeds, thoroughly clear and sanitize the entire storage space. Following standard store preparation steps helps eliminate lingering insect activity and dirt from floor cracks before new bags arrive.

How can you spot spoiled animal feed early?

Regular inspection of your feed stacks allows you to catch spoilage before it affects your entire stock or impacts animal health. Inspect your bags at least once or twice a week, checking both the outer packaging and the physical condition of the feed inside opened bags. Look closely for physical changes, unusual textures, or changes in colour.

Clumping or caking is typically the first visible sign of excess moisture inside a bag. If pellets or mash form hard lumps that do not break apart easily under light pressure, moisture has entered the bag. Damp feed often develops a sour, musty, or stale odor long before discoloured black, green, or white fungal spots become visible to the eye.

Never feed discoloured, caked, or foul-smelling feed to your livestock. If you observe signs of digestive problems or reduced feed consumption among your animals, isolate the suspicious feed batch immediately. Always consult a qualified veterinarian or agricultural extension officer for advice on animal health and safe feed management practices when spoilage is suspected.

Frequently asked questions

Why should animal feed never sit directly on a concrete floor?

Concrete floors pull moisture from the ground through capillary action. This dampness moves directly into paper or woven feed bags, encouraging feed mould growth and rapidly ruining your livestock feed.

What is the first-in, first-out method for feed storage?

First-in, first-out means using your oldest stock of feed before opening newly purchased bags. Date every bag upon arrival and place older bags at the front of your storage pallets so they are consumed first.

Can moldy feed be safe if it is dried in the sun?

Sun-drying moldy feed may reduce surface moisture, but it does not remove harmful toxins produced by fungal growth. Always discard spoiled feed and consult an agricultural extension officer or veterinarian for advice on animal safety.

How does good ventilation help maintain feed quality?

Good airflow prevents heat accumulation and humidity buildup inside the storage shed. Proper ventilation keeps the ambient air dry, reducing condensation on bag surfaces and slowing down fungal growth.
