The desert locust is one of the most damaging migratory pests in the world. It normally lives alone in deserts, but after good rains it breeds fast, forms bands of wingless hoppers and then flying swarms that eat crops and pasture. Kenya saw its worst locust infestation in about 70 years in 2020, with swarms reaching dozens of counties, including Samburu, Marsabit, Isiolo, Meru and Laikipia.

Swarm control is run by county disaster teams, national agencies and international partners, often with aerial spraying and ground surveillance. A single farm cannot stop a large swarm. What a farm can do is watch for early signs, report them quickly and protect its own crops with advice from an agricultural officer.

Types of locusts and grasshoppers found in Kenya

Desert locust (Schistocerca gregaria)

The migratory locust that forms huge swarms. Young hoppers are wingless and move in bands before they grow wings and fly in swarms.

Variegated grasshopper (Zonocerus variegatus)

A brightly coloured grasshopper that feeds on vegetables, cassava and many crops in parts of Africa, especially in warm, wet regions.

Other grasshoppers

Several grasshopper species damage pasture, maize, sorghum, millet and vegetables, especially in dry spells.

Signs of locusts and grasshoppers

  • Dense bands of small wingless hoppers moving across the ground
  • Large clouds of flying insects landing on crops and trees
  • Plants stripped of leaves, with a heavy fall of droppings
  • Egg pods laid in bare sandy soil, which later hatch into hoppers
  • News of swarms in neighbouring counties or from county government and agriculture offices
  • Sudden loss of pasture or crop in a day

Why it matters

Swarms can strip fields in hours and threaten food security and livelihoods. Spraying by aircraft and ground teams is the main control method for large swarms, and can affect livestock, bees and water if used carelessly, so farmers should keep animals away from sprayed areas as advised by officers.

What attracts locusts and grasshoppers

  • Good rains in desert and semi-arid breeding areas, which allow rapid breeding
  • Wind that carries swarms across borders and into new counties
  • Bare sandy soil for egg laying and green vegetation to feed hoppers
  • Delays in reporting and responding to the first bands and swarms
  • Dry spells that push grasshoppers onto crops

How to prevent locusts and grasshoppers

  • Report bands, swarms and egg-laying sites immediately to your county agriculture or administration office
  • Scout your fields often during rains and in the weeks after
  • Follow instructions from agriculture officers and county teams on spraying and keeping people and animals away
  • Do not use unknown chemicals on crops or near water, and always read the label
  • Protect water sources, beehives and fish ponds when spraying is done nearby
  • Keep records of crop loss and photos to help any support or claims process

Frequently asked questions about locusts and grasshoppers

Who should I tell when I see locusts?

Tell your county agriculture or administration office straight away. They coordinate with national agencies and responders.

Can a farm control a locust swarm alone?

Not a large swarm. Early reporting and the response by county and national teams are essential. Farms can protect crops with advice from officers.

Are grasshoppers the same as locusts?

Locusts are a type of grasshopper that forms swarms. Ordinary grasshoppers still damage crops, and need scouting and sometimes control.

Is spraying safe for livestock and bees?

Only if done as the label and the officers advise. Keep animals away from sprayed areas and protect water sources and beehives.
