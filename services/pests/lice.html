Lice are tiny wingless insects that live on people or animals and feed on blood. Each kind lives on one kind of host: human head lice live on human scalps, and poultry and livestock lice live on their own animals. They spread mainly by close contact, though shared brushes, hats, bedding and school dormitory beds can help them travel.

For people, the main treatment is medicated lotion or shampoo from a pharmacy or health worker, together with careful combing. That is not a pest control service. What we can do is treat the surroundings in boarding schools, dormitories, hostels and children's homes, and advise on washing and bagging linen so that lice are not passed on.

Types of lice found in Kenya

Head lice (Pediculus humanus capitis)

Live on the scalp and glue their eggs (nits) to hair near the skin. They spread by head-to-head contact and occasionally on shared combs, hats and bedding. They are an annoyance, not a sign of poor hygiene.

Body lice (Pediculus humanus humanus)

Live in clothing and bedding and move to the skin to feed. They are linked with crowded living conditions where washing clothes and bedding is difficult, and can spread some diseases in those conditions.

Pubic lice

Found on coarse body hair and spread by close contact. A health worker should advise on treatment.

Poultry and livestock lice

Chewing and sucking lice that live on chickens, cattle, goats and other animals, causing scratching, poor growth and reduced production.

Signs of lice

  • Constant itching of the scalp, neck and behind the ears
  • Tiny white or yellowish nits stuck firmly to hair close to the scalp
  • Live lice moving quickly in the hair, which are small and hard to see
  • Scratch marks and sores on the neck and scalp
  • Itchy skin, red marks and lice or eggs in the seams of clothes, for body lice
  • Poultry or livestock rubbing, losing feathers or hair, or losing weight

Why it matters

Head lice do not carry disease, but the itch and scratching disturb sleep and concentration and can lead to sores. In schools, they spread fast and can cause children to miss lessons. Body lice matter more where people live in crowded conditions with limited washing facilities. Anyone with persistent itching or sores should see a health worker or pharmacist.

What attracts lice

  • Close contact in classrooms, dormitories and play
  • Shared brushes, combs, hats, scarves, towels and bedding
  • Crowded sleeping spaces with shared mattresses
  • Limited washing facilities for clothes and bedding
  • Contact between infested and healthy poultry or livestock

How to prevent lice

  • Teach children not to share combs, hats, scarves or hair accessories
  • Check heads regularly with a fine-toothed comb, especially after school holidays
  • Treat everyone who is infested at the same time, using a pharmacy or health worker's advice
  • Wash bedding, towels and clothes hot and dry them on high heat or in strong sun, or seal them in a bag for two weeks
  • Vacuum or brush mattresses and upholstered furniture
  • Keep poultry houses clean and dry and treat infested birds as your veterinarian advises

Frequently asked questions about lice

Are head lice a sign of poor hygiene?

No. Head lice spread by contact and live on clean hair as well as dirty hair.

How long do lice live away from the head?

Only a short time, often a day or two. So the main job is treating the person and washing recently used bedding and hats.

Can you treat children for lice?

No. Children should be treated using a pharmacy or health worker's advice. We treat the dormitory and shared items.

Do you treat poultry lice?

We help farms through our agrovet service, with advice on cleaning, housing and products. Treatment of the birds follows your veterinarian's advice.
