Mosquitoes lay eggs in standing water, and the larvae live in it until they turn into flying adults. A bucket, a blocked gutter, an old tyre or a puddle in a plot can produce thousands of mosquitoes in a week or two. So good mosquito control is half detective work: find and fix the water, then treat the places where adults rest.

Kenya has three groups of mosquitoes that matter to people. Anopheles carry malaria. Aedes, including Aedes aegypti, can spread dengue and chikungunya and bite in the daytime. Culex are a nuisance biter and can carry some other diseases, such as the filarial worm that causes lymphatic filariasis in parts of the coast.

Types of mosquitoes found in Kenya

Anopheles mosquitoes

The mosquitoes that carry malaria. They bite mainly at night, breed in clean sunlit pools, ponds, rice fields and puddles, and are most important around the lake and coastal regions and during rainy seasons in other areas.

Aedes mosquitoes (including Aedes aegypti)

Black and white striped, small, and active in the daytime. They breed in small containers such as pots, tyres, buckets, water tanks and blocked drains, often right inside compounds.

Culex mosquitoes

Common house mosquitoes that bite at night and breed in dirty water, such as drains, septic areas and blocked ditches. They are a nuisance, and in some places they carry other diseases.

Signs of mosquitoes

  • Mosquitoes biting in the evening, at night or during the day indoors
  • Wriggling larvae in water in buckets, tanks, tyres, gutters, drains and puddles
  • Dense adult mosquitoes resting in dark, cool corners, under beds and behind curtains
  • Standing water that stays for more than about a week
  • A rise in biting after heavy rain
  • Several people in a household getting fever or illness after bites, which needs a health worker

Health risks and why it matters

Mosquito-borne diseases cause real illness in Kenya. Malaria risk is highest in the lake and coastal regions and varies in the highlands, and dengue and chikungunya have affected coastal towns. Anyone with fever should get a test and treatment at a clinic, and not treat themselves. Mosquito control for a home, school or estate reduces bites but does not replace a net, repellents and prompt medical care.

What attracts mosquitoes

  • Uncovered water tanks, drums and buckets
  • Tyres, tins, flower pots, plant saucers and containers that collect rain
  • Blocked gutters, drains and ditches with stagnant water
  • Overgrown vegetation that gives adults shade to rest in
  • Swamps, ponds and irrigation channels close to homes

How to prevent mosquitoes

  • Empty, scrub and cover water containers every week, and keep tank lids tight
  • Remove or puncture old tyres and tins, and clear gutters and drains
  • Fill puddles and level ground where water stands
  • Use window and door screens and sleep under a treated net where malaria is a risk
  • Use repellents as the label says and wear long sleeves at dusk
  • Cut back dense bush and grass near the house

Frequently asked questions about mosquitoes

Is fogging enough to stop mosquitoes?

No. It knocks down adults for a short time. Without removing breeding water, they return. We combine fogging with a breeding site check and drainage advice.

When should I get a malaria test?

If you or a child has a fever, go to a clinic and get tested and treated. Do not wait. Mosquito control reduces bites but does not replace testing.

Do mosquitoes breed in clean water?

Yes. Many do, including the Aedes mosquitoes that breed in small clean containers. Cover or empty any container that holds water for more than a week.
