Termites feed on wood, paper and other plant material, and they usually do it out of sight. By the time a door frame sounds hollow or a roof beam sags, a colony may have worked inside it for a long time. In Kenya they damage house timbers, roof trusses, furniture, fences, books, and crops and trees on farms.

Different termites need different handling. Subterranean termites live in the soil and reach buildings through mud tubes. Drywood termites live inside the wood itself and are especially linked with coastal areas. Mound-building termites build the large mounds seen on many Kenyan farms and plots. We inspect first so that we treat the right source.

Types of termites found in Kenya

Subterranean termites

They nest underground and travel through the soil, building mud-covered shelter tubes to cross open surfaces. Their colonies can be large, so they usually cause the most serious damage.

Drywood termites

They live inside the wood they eat in small colonies and leave tiny waste pellets called frass. Winged reproductives leaving to start new nests are one sign. They are especially common near the coast.

Mound-building termites

Species such as Macrotermes and Odontotermes build large mounds and tunnels in the soil and can feed on timber, trees and crops near a plot or a farm house.

Flying termites (swarmers)

Winged adults often leave the colony after the first heavy rains and drop their wings near lights. Discarded wings on windowsills are a sign that a colony is close.

Signs of termites

  • Mud tubes running up foundations, walls or timber
  • Wood that sounds hollow when tapped, or paint that bubbles or blisters over timber
  • Tiny pellet-like droppings below a wooden surface, which can mean drywood termites
  • Discarded wings on windowsills or floors after rain
  • Doors and windows that start to stick, or floors that sag, because timber is being weakened
  • Soil mounds or tunnels near the house, fences or trees

What damage to expect

Termites do not harm people directly, but they weaken timber, damage furniture, documents and stored goods, and can cost a lot to repair if they are left alone. Damage is often hidden behind plaster, ceilings and cupboards, so a termite check is worth doing before buying or renting a property and again whenever you see mud tubes or swarmers.

What attracts termites

  • Wood in contact with soil, such as door frames, fence posts and stored timber
  • Leaking pipes, damp walls and poor drainage that keep soil and wood moist
  • Old tree stumps, roots and buried wood near the building
  • Building without a soil treatment under the foundation or slab
  • A nearby mound or a neighbouring infested building

How to prevent termites

  • Keep timber, cardboard and firewood off the ground and away from walls
  • Repair leaks, gutters and drainage so the soil beside the walls stays dry
  • Remove old stumps, roots and timber offcuts from the plot
  • Treat the soil under new buildings before the slab is cast
  • Inspect door frames, skirting and roof timber once a year, and after heavy rains
  • Do not ignore mud tubes: scraping them away does not remove the colony

Frequently asked questions about termites

How can I tell if termites are in my roof timber?

Look for mud tubes, hollow-sounding wood, tiny pellets below beams and wings after rain. A technician can check roof timber safely and tell you the extent of the damage.

Is scraping off mud tubes enough?

No. Termites rebuild the tubes, and the colony stays where it is. Keep the tubes as evidence for the inspection and book a check.

Do I need to move out during treatment?

Often not. The technician tells you which rooms to keep clear, how long to stay out and how to air the rooms afterwards.

Can termites be prevented in a new building?

Yes. Treating the soil before the slab is cast, keeping timber off the ground, and fixing leaks early reduce the risk a great deal. Ask us before the foundation is covered.
