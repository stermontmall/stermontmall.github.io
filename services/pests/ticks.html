Ticks are small eight-legged blood-feeders related to spiders. They climb onto animals from grass, bush and floors, attach, feed for days and drop off to lay eggs. A tick that drops off a dog in a house can lay thousands of eggs in cracks and skirting, which is how one pet can lead to a house full of ticks.

In Kenya, ticks matter most on farms. Tick-borne diseases such as East Coast fever are a major cause of cattle losses, and ticks also cause wounds, weight loss and damaged hides. On the home side, the brown dog tick lives indoors in kennels, cracks and wall crevices.

Types of ticks found in Kenya

Brown dog tick (Rhipicephalus sanguineus)

A common household tick that can complete its whole life indoors and infest kennels, rooms and walls where a dog lives.

Brown ear tick (Rhipicephalus appendiculatus)

A cattle tick in much of Kenya and the carrier of the parasite that causes East Coast fever.

Tropical bont tick (Amblyomma variegatum)

A large, brightly marked tick on cattle, sheep and goats. It causes painful bites that can become infected and spreads other livestock disease.

Blue ticks (Rhipicephalus microplus)

A one-host cattle tick that spends its whole life on one animal and is a known cause of tick fever and anaemia.

Signs of ticks

  • Small grey or brown swollen bumps on the skin of dogs, cattle, goats and sheep, often around the ears, neck, belly and between the legs
  • Ticks in or around kennels, bedding, cracks in walls and skirting
  • Pets scratching and chewing at the same spots
  • Livestock with weight loss, fever, swollen lymph glands or reduced milk yield, which need a veterinary check
  • Small ticks climbing walls, curtains and ceilings in rooms where a dog lives
  • Itchy bite marks on people after walking through long grass or visiting farms

Health risks and why it matters

Ticks spread diseases that can kill livestock, such as East Coast fever, and some can spread illnesses to people, for example African tick bite fever. If a person develops fever, a rash or an eschar (a dark scab at the bite) after a tick bite, they should see a health worker. For dairy and beef farmers, tick control is a cost-saving task: it protects animals, milk and hides.

What attracts ticks

  • Long grass, bush and shrubs where ticks wait for animals
  • Dogs that are not treated, or pets that roam and mix with wildlife and livestock
  • Livestock sharing pasture with infested animals or with wild animals
  • Cracks, crevices and wooden walls in kennels and houses where tick eggs are laid
  • Irregular dipping or spraying of livestock

How to prevent ticks

  • Keep grass cut short around the house and around animal shelters
  • Treat pets as advised by your veterinarian and check them regularly, especially around the ears and between the toes
  • Remove ticks with fine tweezers close to the skin and pull steadily, without twisting or crushing them
  • Follow a regular dipping or spraying schedule for livestock, using products as the label and your veterinarian or livestock officer direct
  • Seal wall cracks and clean kennels and bedding often
  • Rotate pastures where you can and avoid sharing grazing with unknown animals

Frequently asked questions about ticks

How do I remove a tick safely?

Use fine tweezers, grip the tick close to the skin and pull steadily without twisting or crushing it. Clean the area and wash your hands. If a rash or fever follows, see a health worker.

Can ticks live inside the house?

Yes. The brown dog tick can complete its whole life indoors where dogs live and lay eggs in cracks and skirting boards.

How often should cattle be sprayed or dipped?

It depends on the season, the tick species and the product, so follow your veterinarian or livestock officer and the product label.
