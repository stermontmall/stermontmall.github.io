Insects can destroy a large share of a harvest in storage. Maize, beans, sorghum, rice, wheat flour, dried cassava and pulses are attacked by a handful of beetles and moths that breed inside the grain, so the damage is often hidden until the grain is full of holes and flour. For farmers, traders, schools and millers, store pests mean lost food, lost income and grain that may no longer be safe to sell or eat.

Damaged and damp grain also grows moulds, and some moulds produce aflatoxins that are harmful to health. So good storage is both a food loss issue and a food safety issue. The most reliable protection is a combination: harvest on time, dry well, clean and repair the store, use airtight or hermetic storage, check regularly and treat when needed.

Types of stored grain pests found in Kenya

Maize weevil (Sitophilus zeamais)

A small dark snout beetle that lays eggs inside kernels. The larvae feed inside, and adults emerge through round holes. It attacks maize, rice, sorghum and other grains.

Larger grain borer (Prostephanus truncatus)

A cylindrical dark beetle that bores into maize and dried cassava, producing a lot of flour-like dust. It can cause rapid losses in stored maize and cassava chips and is a major pest in East Africa.

Angoumois grain moth (Sitotroga cerealella)

A small buff moth whose larvae feed inside kernels in the field and store, leaving round exit holes and webbing.

Bean bruchids (bean weevils)

Small beetles that lay eggs on beans and other pulses. The larvae feed inside the seed and adults emerge through small round holes.

Flour beetles and grain beetles

Small reddish-brown beetles that attack broken grain, flour and meal and give it a bad smell. They often follow the main pests in dirty or damp stores.

Signs of stored grain pests

  • Small round holes in grain, and kernels that feel light or hollow
  • Fine flour-like dust at the bottom of sacks or bins
  • Live weevils, beetles or small moths on sacks, walls and floors
  • Clumped, warm or damp grain with a musty smell
  • Webbing and clumps in flour and meal
  • Grain that was clean at harvest becoming damaged within weeks

Why it matters

Storage pests reduce the weight, quality and price of grain, and they open the way for moulds. Aflatoxin contamination has caused serious illness in Kenya in the past, so do not eat visibly mouldy grain, and do not feed it to animals. Traders and millers also face rejected loads when grain is infested.

What attracts stored grain pests

  • Harvesting late, or storing grain that is not dry enough
  • Old grain and dust left in the store, sacks and bins from the previous season
  • Infested grain mixed with a new harvest, or insects already in the field crop
  • Cracks, gaps and open roofs that let in pests, rodents and moisture
  • Leaving grain in open sacks, in damp rooms or on the floor

How to prevent stored grain pests

  • Harvest on time and dry the grain well before storage: the figure commonly given for maize is about 13.5 per cent moisture
  • Clean, sweep and repair the store before the new harvest, and remove old grain and dust
  • Use airtight or hermetic bags and containers, which deprive insects of air
  • Keep sacks on pallets and away from walls, and keep rodents out
  • Inspect the grain every few weeks for holes, dust and live insects
  • Do not mix old and new grain, and use any chemical treatment only as the label directs

Frequently asked questions about stored grain pests

Can I still use maize that has weevil holes?

Insect-damaged grain is lower quality and more likely to have moulds. Do not use grain that smells musty or is visibly mouldy, and do not feed it to animals. Ask a health or agriculture officer if in doubt.

Are hermetic bags worth it?

They are a widely recommended way to protect grain without relying on chemicals alone, if the grain is dry and the bags are undamaged. Ask us about options.

When should I treat the store?

Before the new harvest goes in. Clean and treat the empty store, then keep the grain dry and check it regularly.
